The women of Qatar played essential roles in sustaining families and communities,
particularly during the months when men were away on pearl diving expeditions. Umm Khalid,
born in Al Wakrah in 1932, shared her memories:

"When the men left for the diving season, we women became everything — mother, father,
provider, protector. My mother would weave fishing nets and sew sails to earn money. She
taught me Al Sadu weaving when I was just seven years old. The patterns we wove told our
stories — the diamond shapes represented the tents of our ancestors, and the zigzag lines
represented the waves of the sea that took our men away."

Women were the keepers of traditional knowledge. They practiced herbal medicine, using
plants from the desert like Sidr (Ziziphus) for healing wounds and Al Henna for celebrations.
Midwives, known as Daya, were respected figures in every community. They delivered babies
and cared for mothers using knowledge passed down through generations.

During the pearling season, women gathered at the shore each evening, scanning the horizon
for returning dhows. They maintained the household, managed finances, and made critical
decisions in the absence of their husbands. The farewell and return ceremonies were deeply
emotional events marked by poetry, song, and communal solidarity.

Traditional Qatari women were also artisans of great skill. They created intricate gold
thread embroidery called Telli, which adorned thobes and ceremonial garments. The art of
Al Naqda (gold thread work on fabric) was particularly valued and remains an important
part of Qatar's intangible cultural heritage.

The discovery of oil brought rapid changes to women's lives. Education became available,
and in 1956, the first girls' school opened in Doha. Sheikha Moza bint Nasser has since
championed education and women's empowerment, establishing Education City and the Qatar
Foundation. Today, Qatari women hold prominent positions in government, business, academia,
and the arts — a transformation achieved within living memory.
